# Perspective Manipulation, Dual Protagonists, and the Limits of Interactive Empathy

## 1. Theoretical Grounding: The Architecture of Empathy in Games

Empathy in digital games has frequently been theorized through **avatar incorporation** (Calleja, 2011) and the tripartite model of the **Player-Character Relationship (PCR)** (Gee, 2003): the player, the virtual character, and the hybrid "projective identity" formed at their intersection. In conventional action-adventure narratives, games cultivate a stable, uncritical alignment between player agency and avatar morality. Players are encouraged to identify unconditionally with the protagonist’s goals, treating enemies as morally vacant obstacles.

*The Last of Us Part II* deliberately shatters this contract. Rather than treating empathy as a passive byproduct of cinematic identification, Naughty Dog constructs empathy as a **site of acute ludonarrative friction**. By forcing the player to inhabit two mutually antagonistic consciousnesses—first Ellie, then Abby—the game executes an aggressive psychological experiment in **perspective engineering**.

---

## 2. The Midpoint Shock and Narrative Inversion

The structural pivot of *Part II* occurs at the conclusion of Seattle Day 3. For approximately 15 to 18 hours, the player has been conditioned into Ellie’s subjective grief and bloodlust. Every enemy slaughtered, every dog killed, and every safe cracked has been performed in service of avenging Joel.

At the exact moment of climactic confrontation in the Seattle theater—Abby kicks open the door, executes Jesse, shoots Tommy through the skull, and aims her pistol directly at Ellie—the narrative abruptly arrests the climax. A black screen gives way to a title card: **Seattle Day 1 — Abby**.

```
[Hours 0–16: Ellie's Retributive Vector] ──> [THEATER CLIFFHANGER] ──> [Hours 16–28: Abby's Redemptive Vector]
(Alignment: Monolithic Grief & Rage)        (Radical Narrative Shock)  (Alignment: Cognitive Dissonance & Friction)
```

### The Psychological Resistance to the Switch
This transition provokes what media psychologists term **epistemological revulsion**. Players are not simply asked to watch Abby; they are forced to *become* her. They must run, climb, craft, upgrade, and fight using the somatic motor inputs of the woman who violently smashed Joel’s skull with a golf club.

In their seminal empirical study of player responses to this switch, Erb, Lee, and Doh (2021) conducted semi-structured qualitative interviews with 12 players, uncovering three core psychological variables that determine whether a player accepts or rejects this forced alignment:

1. **Tolerance of Forced Character Switch:** The player's willingness to surrender ludic control over narrative direction. Players with low tolerance experienced the switch as a personal betrayal by the developers, resulting in disengagement or hostile speed-running.
2. **Malleability of Character Image:** The cognitive flexibility to revise established moral judgments in the light of retroactive counter-evidence. Players who viewed Abby strictly through a static "villain" archetype experienced severe confirmation bias, refusing to acknowledge her virtues.
3. **Flexibility of Character Attachment:** The capacity to suspend parasocial devotion to Joel and Ellie in order to entertain an alternative perspective. Players with rigid attachment experienced intense resentment, viewing every sympathetic depiction of Abby as emotional manipulation.

## 3. Cognitive vs. Affective Empathy

A critical contribution of Erb et al.’s (2021) findings—supported by broader psychological models of social cognition—is the vital distinction between **cognitive empathy** and **affective empathy**:

| Dimension | Psychological Definition | Manifestation in *The Last of Us Part II* | Player Response Profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive Empathy** | The intellectual capacity to comprehend another person's internal mental state, reasoning, and motivations (*perspective-taking*). | The player learns that Jerry Anderson was Abby's father; that Joel slaughtered her community; that she spared Ellie and Tommy in the chalet; that her friends were hunted down. | **High Achievability:** Over 85% of players intellectually grasp *why* Abby killed Joel and recognize that from her perspective, Joel was a monstrous mass murderer. |
| **Affective Empathy** | The emotional capacity to feel *with* another person; sharing their somatic grief, warmth, affection, and distress (*compassionate resonance*). | The player is asked to feel joy when Abby plays fetch with Alice the dog, romantic longing during her moments with Owen, or maternal warmth toward Lev. | **Severe Polarization:** A significant proportion of players report an inability or outright refusal to feel affection for Abby, experiencing her sympathetic scenes as heavy-handed authorial coercion. |

This divide exposes the central theoretical flaw in Naughty Dog's design hypothesis: **cognitive understanding does not necessitate emotional forgiveness**. A player can fully understand why Abby exacted retribution upon Joel while simultaneously despising her for doing so.

## 4. The Parallel Seattle Architecture: Mirroring and Moral Inversion

The brilliance of the game's spatial and temporal design lies in its **parallel day structure**. Both women spend three days in the rain-soaked ruins of Seattle, but their moral and emotional vectors move in precisely opposite directions:

```
MORAL VECTOR COMPARISON: SEATTLE DAYS 1 TO 3

Day 1:
  - Ellie: Arrives hopeful, accompanied by Dina; open exploration; sings "Take on Me" (Descent begins).
  - Abby: Trapped in militarized WLF routine; alienated from Owen; captured by Seraphites (Nadir of isolation).

Day 2:
  - Ellie: Solitary hunt through Hillcrest; infiltrates hospital; beats Nora to death with a pipe (Descent into darkness).
  - Abby: Goes AWOL to save Lev and Yara; descends to hospital Ground Zero; slaughters the Rat King for supplies (Ascent into care).

Day 3:
  - Ellie: Steals boat in typhoon; infiltrates Aquarium; murders Owen and pregnant Mel; moral collapse (Total spiritual exhaustion).
  - Abby: Scales sky bridges with Lev; survives burning of Haven; breaks with WLF; protects Lev (Moral awakening through protection).
```

### The Hermeneutic Retrospect of Violence
Every major encounter in Abby’s campaign retroactively reconfigures actions the player performed as Ellie:
- **Alice the Dog:** As Ellie, the player was ambushed in the aquarium and forced in a quick-time event to stab an aggressive guard dog to death. As Abby, the player discovers that this dog is Alice, a joyful, loyal companion who fetches squeaky toys and leans against Abby’s legs for comfort.
- **The Aquarium Sanctuary:** As Ellie, the aquarium is an ominous, darkened fortress harboring conspirators. As Abby, it is revealed to be Owen's whimsical sanctuary, decorated with string lights, an abandoned sailboat, and a children’s drawing board.
- **Mel and Owen:** As Ellie, Mel and Owen are hostile conspirators who refuse to surrender Abby’s location. As Abby, the player witnesses the agonizing, intimate reality of Mel’s pregnancy, her justified moral doubts about Abby's brutality, and Owen's desperate, idealistic dream of escaping the senseless slaughter.

## 5. The Theater Boss Fight: The Ultimate Ludo-Affective Double-Bind

The culmination of the game's perspective manipulation occurs when the syuzhet catches up to the theater confrontation. Having humanized Abby across 12 hours of gameplay, **the game does not return control to Ellie. Instead, the player must control Abby in a life-or-death boss battle against Ellie.**

```
                     ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                     │          THE THEATER CONFRONTATION (BOSS)        │
                     └─────────────────────────┬────────────────────────┘
                                               │
               ┌───────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┐
               ▼                                                               ▼
 [Player Motor Control: ABBY]                                    [AI Controlled Antagonist: ELLIE]
   - Forced to stalk, dodge, and pummel                            - Uses the player's own tactics:
   - High somatic vulnerability                                      trip-mines, shotgun blasts, stealth
   - Moral distress: Hurting the beloved avatar                    - Staged as a terrifying, feral monster
```

### Player Subversion and Refusal to Fight
This encounter generates the most extreme **moral distress** documented in modern gaming (Anderson, 2022). Players find themselves mechanically weaponized against the character they have protected since 2013.
- Empirical observations and player testimonies document widespread instances of **deliberate gameplay refusal**: players intentionally refused to press attack buttons, allowed Ellie to shoot or stab Abby, or triggered repeated game-overs as an act of ludic protest against the authorial script.
- The game stages Ellie not as a heroic avenger, but as a terrifying, feral boss monster who creeps through the shadows with shotguns, machetes, and stun bombs. The player experiences firsthand the terror of being hunted by the very protagonist whose stealth lethal skills they mastered in the preceding chapters.

When Abby pins a pregnant Dina to the floor and places a knife to her throat, declaring *"Good"* in response to Ellie’s desperate plea, the player is brought to the brink of utter moral revulsion. Only the intervention of Lev—an innocent child whose moral purity Abby cannot bear to corrupt—halts the slaughter.

## 6. Critical Evaluation: Genuine Empathy Expansion or Coercive Manipulation?

Does *The Last of Us Part II* genuinely expand interactive empathy, or does it engage in didactic emotional bullying?

### The Case for Authentic Expansion
Proponents (Spence, 2024; Schubert, 2021) argue that the game exploits the unique affordances of interactive media to dismantle tribalism. In literature or film, perspective shifts are intellectual exercises; in a game, **shared motor agency forces somatic co-presence**. By making the player physically survive as Abby—feeling her fear of heights while crossing the crane bridge, feeling her exhaustion, and experiencing her protective love for Lev—the game creates an embodied knowledge that intellectual dialogue alone cannot achieve.

### The Case for Coercive Manipulation
Critics and skeptical scholars (Park, 2021; Erb et al., 2021) point out that the game's empathy pipeline is fundamentally **coercive and asymmetrical**:
1. **The Rigged Dice:** To make Abby sympathetic, the developers give her superior moral redemption arcs (saving orphaned enemy children, fighting a horrific bioweapon to secure medical gear) while systematically stripping Ellie of her charm, humor, and humanity, reducing her to a grim, hollow torturer.
2. **The Denial of Player Agency:** The player is forced to commit atrocities as Ellie (beating Nora, killing Mel) that many players actively did not wish to commit, only for the narrative to sanctimoniously berate the player for those exact actions.
3. **The Mechanical Disparity:** Abby is granted an elite arsenal (military assault rifle, flamethrower, hunting pistol, high-damage pipe bombs), making her mechanically more versatile and thrilling to play than Ellie, using gameplay pleasure to bribe the player into alignment.

### Synthesis: The Imperfect Mirror
Ultimately, *The Last of Us Part II* succeeds not because it forces every player to love Abby, but because it exposes the **contingency and fragility of moral righteousness**. By demonstrating that every character is both the hero of their own survival story and the monstrous antagonist of someone else’s, the game transforms interactive perspective from a vehicle of escapist power into an instrument of profound moral self-interrogation.
