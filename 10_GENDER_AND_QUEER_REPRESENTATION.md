# Gender Performativity, Queer Affect, and Transgender Representation

## 1. Theoretical Grounding: Gender and Queerness in Interactive Media

Critical analysis of gender and sexuality in *The Last of Us Part II* requires navigating the intersection of **feminist game studies** (Shaw, 2014), **queer game studies** (Ruberg, 2019), and **gender performativity** (Butler, 1990). Mainstream AAA video games have historically codified female characters through rigid patriarchal archetypes: hyper-sexualized visual spectacles designed for the heterosexual male gaze, passive damsels functioning as emotional catalysts for male heroism, or sanitized maternal figures.

*The Last of Us Part II* represents one of the most radical, high-budget disruptions of these conventions in gaming history. Centering two female protagonists—one openly lesbian (Ellie), the other a hyper-muscular soldier whose body transgresses conventional feminine ideals (Abby)—and featuring a prominent transgender male co-lead (Lev), the game establishes a mature discursive terrain. 

However, academic evaluation must avoid uncritical cheerleading. Following **Stefan Schubert (2021)**, **Kimberly Dennin and Adrianna Burton (2023)**, and **Aiden J. Kosciesza (2023)**, this chapter interrogates both the transformative achievements and the lingering ideological contradictions embedded in the game's representation of gender, queerness, and trans identity.

## 2. Ellie and Abby: Subverting the Video Game Male Gaze

Neither Ellie nor Abby conforms to the cosmetic or narrative imperatives of mainstream video-game femininity:

```
                            SUBVERSION OF FEMININE TROPES
                                          │
       ┌──────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                                                     ▼
[Ellie Williams]                                                      [Abby Anderson]
- Lesbian identity normalized and central.                            - Hyper-muscular, utilitarian physique.
- Pragmatic, weathered, non-sexualized attire.                        - Direct, brutal pugilistic combat style.
- Emotional vulnerability paired with lethal fury.                    - Subversion of fragile damsel aesthetics.
- Rejection of domestic passivity.                                    - Active sexual agency (relationship with Owen).
```

### Abby's Muscularity and the Politics of Body Policing
Abby’s physical form is perhaps the most politically contested body in modern digital culture. Rather than possessing the slender, stylized curves typical of female action heroines (e.g., Lara Croft, Bayonetta), Abby possesses an imposing, muscular frame reminiscent of an elite Olympic weightlifter or CrossFit athlete.
- **Narrative Contextualization:** Her body is not a decorative gimmick; it is an intradiegetic record of traumatic obsession. The WLF base in the Seattle Seahawks stadium includes fully equipped weight-training gyms and high-protein cafeterias, illustrating the systematic, four-year physical regimen Abby undertook to become physically capable of overpowering Joel.
- **Audience Backlash and Misogynistic Panic:** Following the April 2020 pre-release plot leaks, Abby’s physical appearance became the focal point of intense reactionary hostility. Online forums mobilized bad-faith claims that her physique was "biologically impossible" without anabolic steroids, frequently misgendering her as a trans woman in derogatory terms. As Schubert (2021) documents, this backlash laid bare the deep-seated fragility of traditional gaming demographics when confronted with a female body that utterly refuses heterosexual commodification.
- **Academic Critique (Schubert, 2021):** Schubert critically asks whether Abby’s empowerment is genuinely subversive or merely an assimilation into hyper-masculine metrics of power. By granting Abby agency almost exclusively through physical domination, bone-breaking violence, and militarized ruthlessness, does the game celebrate female liberation, or does it declare that women can only achieve heroic narrative weight by adopting the most destructive behaviors of toxic masculinity?

## 3. Queer Domesticity and "Experiential Play": Ellie and Dina

Ellie and Dina’s relationship represents a landmark portrayal of lesbian intimacy in blockbuster gaming, characterized by what Dennin and Burton (2023) conceptualize as **"experiential play"**:

```
                       AFFECTIVE SPECTRUM OF QUEERNESS
                                      │
       ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                                             ▼
[Empathetic Queerness]                                        [Grating Queerness]
- The intimacy of the music shop ("Take on Me").              - Seth's bigoted slur at the Jackson dance.
- Domestic sanctuary of the Wyoming farmhouse.                - The ambient precarity of post-apocalyptic survival.
- Co-parenting infant JJ; tenderness and warmth.              - The catastrophic cost of Ellie's vengeful obsession.
```

### A. Empathetic Queerness: The Normalization of Lesbian Intimacy
In *The Last of Us Part II*, Ellie's sexuality is neither an exoticized titillation for a straight male audience nor a tragic burden that requires narrative apology.
- In Downtown Seattle, when Ellie finds an acoustic guitar in an abandoned music shop, she sits down and plays an intimate, stripped-down acoustic rendition of A-ha’s *"Take on Me"* for Dina. The scene is tender, joyful, and deeply romantic, framed with genuine affection.
- In the farmhouse chapter, the game allocates hours of quiet, domestic gameplay to Ellie, Dina, and baby JJ. The player engages in mundane, loving tasks: fetching clothes from the line, slow-dancing in the kitchen to vinyl records, and soothing a crying infant. Dina is depicted as an articulate, humorous, and emotionally mature partner who offers Ellie an authentic, generative future beyond trauma.

### B. Grating Queerness: Structural Dread and Societal Friction
However, Dennin and Burton (2023) emphasize that *Part II* never allows queer joy to exist in untroubled isolation:
- At the winter dance in Jackson, when Ellie and Dina kiss, an elderly town resident named Seth aggressively confronts them, calling Ellie a *"dyke"* and causing a public altercation. This scene demonstrates that even in a tightly knit, egalitarian survivor community like Jackson, historical heteronormative bigotry persists.
- Furthermore, Ellie’s inability to accept domestic bliss—abandoning Dina and JJ in the middle of the night to pursue Abby to Santa Barbara—illustrates how unresolved retributive hatred actively dismantles queer kinship. Dina’s departure from the farmhouse is an act of supreme self-respect; she refuses to be the passive, endlessly waiting wife to Ellie’s self-destructive crusade.

## 4. Lev and the "Moral Service" of Transgender Representation

The introduction of **Lev (voiced by transgender actor Ian Alexander)** represents a historic milestone: the first prominent, nuanced transgender co-lead in a major AAA production. However, his characterization sits at the center of a profound academic debate regarding the instrumentalization of marginalized bodies.

```
                           LEV'S NARRATIVE PARADOX
                                      │
       ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                                             ▼
[Groundbreaking Representation]                               [The "Moral Service" Critique]
- Voiced by trans actor Ian Alexander.                        - Lev's trauma fuels Abby's moral redemption.
- Trans identity organically tied to Seraphite culture.       - Extreme physical and psychological suffering.
- Authentic male self-determination.                          - Limited narrative agency independent of Abby.
```

### A. Cultural Context: The Seraphite Tradition and Apostasy
Lev’s gender identity is deeply integrated into the sociological world-building of the **Seraphites (or Scars)**, a religious fundamentalist cult living on Haven island off the coast of Seattle:
- Born biologically female and named Lily, Lev was assigned to become the child-bride of a senior Seraphite elder.
- In a radical act of bodily autonomy and spiritual defiance, Lev shaved his head—a ritual physical marker reserved strictly for male warriors—and proclaimed himself a boy named Lev.
- This act of self-determination branded him an apostate, leading to his brutal persecution by his community and his own mother, forcing his sister Yara to flee with him into exile.

### B. The "Moral Service" Critique (Kosciesza, 2023)
While recognizing the dignity of Ian Alexander's performance and the narrative validity of Lev’s faith, **Aiden J. Kosciesza (2023)** advances a rigorous critique of Lev's structural function within the game:
1. **The Instrumentalization of Trans Suffering:** Kosciesza argues that video-game narratives frequently place trans NPCs into **"moral service"**—using their victimhood, vulnerability, and systemic persecution as an ethical whetstone upon which a morally compromised cisgender protagonist (Abby) can sharpen their conscience and achieve redemption.
2. **The Asymmetry of Agency:** Abby risks everything to save Lev, and Lev repeatedly acts as Abby’s moral compass (most crucially stopping her from slitting Dina’s throat in the theater). However, Lev’s own narrative agency is tragically truncated:
   - When Lev returns to Haven to save his mother, his mother attacks him in religious hysteria, and Lev accidentally kills her in self-defense.
   - His sister Yara is gunned down by WLF soldiers.
   - By the story's end, Lev has lost his family, his home, and his religion, existing entirely as an appendage to Abby’s survival trajectory.

## 5. Critical Synthesis: Beyond Binary Paradigms

*The Last of Us Part II* cannot be classified cleanly as an uncompromised feminist triumph or a reactionary exploitation of marginalized trauma. Its true significance lies in its refusal to offer sanitized, idealized representation.

By allowing female characters to be simultaneously loving, muscular, brutal, grieving, and profoundly flawed, the game grants women the **full, messy spectrum of human moral complexity**. Similarly, while Lev's narrative arc is burdened by disproportionate tragedy and moral service, his presence permanently shatters the cis-heteronormative assumptions of the post-apocalyptic genre. The game demonstrates that in the ruins of civilization, gender and sexuality are not erased; rather, the struggle for bodily autonomy, queer intimacy, and self-definition becomes the ultimate frontier of human survival.
