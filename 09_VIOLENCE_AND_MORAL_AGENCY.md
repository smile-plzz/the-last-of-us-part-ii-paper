# Playable Violence, Procedural Rhetoric, and the Limits of Moral Agency

## 1. Theoretical Grounding: Procedural Rhetoric and the Ethics of Play

To analyze violence in *The Last of Us Part II*, we must draw upon the theoretical intersection of **procedural rhetoric** (Bogost, 2007) and the **ethics of computer games** (Sicart, 2009, 2014). Bogost argues that video games do not convey arguments primarily through words or moving images, but through **computational rules, behavioral constraints, and systemic affordances**. Sicart expands this by establishing that a player acts as an autonomous moral subject within an ethically structured decision space.

In *The Last of Us Part II*, however, the ethical design is marked by a profound structural paradox. The narrative vehemently condemns retributive violence as an intoxicating, self-destroying sickness. Yet, the core ludic systems—the moment-to-moment gameplay loops—require the player to execute hyper-efficient, visceral slaughter in order to advance the story. This chapter interrogates how the game's mechanics construct an embodied rhetoric of violence, and whether this design fosters genuine moral reflection or collapses into hypocritical moralization.

## 2. The Ludic Systems of Lethality: Tactile Procedural Rhetoric

Naughty Dog’s combat engine is engineered to strip violence of the sanitized, frictionless abstractions typical of mainstream action blockbusters. Every mechanic is designed to heighten the somatic, physical weight of inflicting bodily harm:

```
                          TACTILE ANATOMY OF VIOLENCE IN PART II
                                             │
      ┌──────────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┐
      ▼                                      ▼                                      ▼
[Resource Scarcity & Crafting]         [Enemy AI Humanization]                [Visceral Motor Animation]
- Real-time backpack crafting         - NPCs call out unique names           - Motion-matching inertia
- Jamming guns & weapon sway           - Panic, surrender, & weeping          - Arterial spray & choking
- Physical weapon bench upgrades      - Companion dogs mourn handlers        - Somatic vibration (haptics)
```

### A. The Tactility of Preparation: The Weapon Upgrade Bench
In conventional games, weapon upgrades occur in static, abstract menus. In *Part II*, upgrading a weapon is a prolonged, physical, and intimate ritual:
- When Ellie or Abby interacts with a workbench, the camera zooms into extreme close-up.
- The character physically unholsters the firearm, sets it on the wooden surface, disassembles the receiver with screwdrivers and rags, cleans the springs, polishes the chamber, and files the metal.
- The sound design amplifies the heavy metallic clicks, scraping sandpaper, and oiling mechanisms.
- **The Procedural Rhetoric:** The game forces the player to intimately contemplate the mechanics of death. Upgrades are not magical stat-boosts; they are explicit, mechanical enhancements engineered to make bullets tear through human flesh and bone with greater efficiency.

### B. Enemy AI Design: Procedural De-anonymization
In standard shooters, non-player character (NPC) enemies are anonymous, interchangeable "grunts" designed to be dispatched without ethical friction. *The Last of Us Part II* systematically dismantles this cognitive defense mechanism:
- **Procedural Naming System:** Every human enemy in the WLF and Seraphite factions is assigned a unique name in the game's memory (e.g., "Gemma," "Omar," "Caleb"). When an enemy is killed from stealth or in open combat, their squadmates react with panic: *"Oh God, they got Omar!"* or *"Gemma, stay with me! Who did this?!"*
- **Somatic Death Agony:** Enemies shot in the lungs or throat do not immediately despawn or ragdoll; they collapse to the floor, gurgling on their own blood, clutching wounds, pleading for their lives, or calling out for their parents and lovers.
- **Surrender and Fear Mechanics:** Injured enemies caught without ammo will drop their weapons, fall to their knees, raise their hands, and beg for mercy. However, if the player hesitates, the enemy may desperately draw a hidden knife, forcing the player into a brutal preemptive kill.

### C. Canine Combat and the Agony of Animal Violence
The introduction of guard dogs in the WLF ranks introduces a uniquely distressing mechanical layer:
- **Scent-Tracking Mechanic:** Dogs leave a visible scent trail in "Listen Mode," forcing the player to constantly keep moving or lay explosive trip-mines.
- **Tactile Defense:** When a dog ambushes the player, it pins Ellie or Abby to the ground, requiring rapid, panicked button-mashing to stab the animal with a knife or fend it off.
- **Auditory Grief:** When a dog is killed, its human handler screams in genuine, unscripted agony (*"Bear! No, no, Bear!"*). Conversely, when a handler is killed, the dog whimpers beside their corpse, refusing to leave their side.
- **Emotional Discomfort:** Players across all demographics reported acute distress regarding the mandatory killing of dogs, highlighting how interactive media triggers moral resistance far more intensely through domesticated animals than through human avatars.

## 3. Contrasting Combat Identities: Ellie vs. Abby

The game does not merely provide two protagonists; it constructs two fundamentally divergent **somatic combat personas** that communicate personality through motor mechanics:

| Combat Dimension | Ellie Williams (The Agility of Vengeance) | Abby Anderson (The Weight of Militarization) |
|---|---|---|
| **Body Dynamics & Movement** | Lightweight, agile, low center of gravity; swift prone crawls through tall grass; fluid squeeze-through gaps. | Heavy, deliberate, explosive kinetic momentum; high physical mass; struggles in narrow spaces. |
| **Melee Philosophy** | Switchblade reliance; quick evasive dodges followed by rapid, puncturing counter-slashes; vulnerability to blunt trauma. | Brutal pugilism; heavy haymakers, hammer strikes, and chokeholds; ability to momentum-chain consecutive melee kills. |
| **Arsenal & Crafting** | Guerilla and improvisor kit: Hunting bow, silencers, explosive trap mines, smoke bombs, tripwires. | Military commando kit: Semi-auto assault rifle, hunting pistol, high-yield pipe bombs, tactical flamethrower. |
| **Brutality Framing** | Feral, frantic, desperate, and increasingly unhinged; screaming curses during combat (*"Die, you fucking piece of shit!"*). | Controlled, ruthless, disciplined military precision; cold tactical efficiency forged by years of WLF conditioning. |
| **Ludic Rhetoric** | The mechanics of a hunted animal that has turned into a rabid predator. | The mechanics of an elite military battering ram discovering the vulnerability of its armor. |

## 4. The De-Optionalization Paradox: Constrained Agency vs. Moral Scolding

The central theoretical controversy of *The Last of Us Part II* lies in what game studies scholars term the **De-Optionalization Paradox**:

```
                       THE DE-OPTIONALIZATION PARADOX
                                     │
      ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
      ▼                                                             ▼
[Ludic Imperative (The Game)]                                 [Narrative Critique (The Story)]
- "Kill everyone to survive and advance."                     - "Violence is evil, corrosive, and hollow."
- Rewards stealth mastery and headshots.                      - Scolds the player for Nora and Mel.
- Denies non-lethal surrender options.                        - Punishes the protagonist with total loss.
```

### The Absence of Non-Lethal Agency
Unlike immersive sims such as *Dishonored*, *Deus Ex*, or *Metal Gear Solid*—which afford players the ludic freedom to complete encounters non-lethally and tailor their moral footprint—*The Last of Us Part II* strictly **de-optionalizes violence**:
- You cannot talk Nora down.
- You cannot tie up Mel and Owen.
- You cannot bypass the final, agonizing drowning fight on the beach.
- To progress the software state to the next chapter, **the player must execute the violent command**.

### Moral Distress vs. Ludo-Affective Disruption
This constraint produces radically different academic interpretations:

1. **The Critical Indictment (Moral Distress / Hypocrisy):** Scholars like Anderson (2022) argue that the game traps the player in an ethical double-bind that borders on artistic bad faith. The game forces the player to participate in horrific acts by withholding alternative mechanical verbs, and then uses cutscenes to chastise the player for being violent. The player is denied *moral agency* but saddled with *moral guilt*.
2. **The Defense (Ludo-Affective Disruption):** Scholars like Park (2021) argue that this friction is precisely the point. If *Part II* offered a non-lethal option, it would allow the player to evade the horror of Ellie's psychological reality. Ellie is not a neutral avatar waiting for the player's ethical guidance; she is a specific, deeply traumatized character whose obsession overrides rational choice. By stripping the player of choice, the game forces the player to feel the **claustrophobic horror of complicity**—experiencing what it feels like to participate in a catastrophic trajectory that you are powerless to halt.

## 5. Interactive Violence and Emotional Responsibility: Does Play Heighten Guilt?

Does the physical requirement of pressing buttons make players feel more ethically culpable for virtual violence, or does repeated exposure merely desensitize them?

### The Motor Act of Murder
The game’s most harrowing sequences require what can be termed **motor-synchronized execution**:
- In the basement of the Seattle hospital, Ellie corners Nora. To force information from her, the game cuts to a cinematic perspective, but **it will not advance automatically**.
- A prompt appears on screen: **[Square]**.
- When the player presses Square, Ellie swings a metal pipe into Nora’s body. Nora screams.
- The prompt reappears: **[Square]**. The player must press it again. And again.
- The game forces the player’s thumb to physically rhythmize the torture. You cannot look away, and you cannot let the controller sit idle; the game demands your kinetic complicity.

### The Limits of Procedural Guilt
However, there is an inherent danger in this design. Across 25 to 30 hours of continuous gameplay, the sheer volume of combat encounters (slaughtering hundreds of WLF soldiers, Seraphites, and infected) can produce **ludic desensitization**. 
- When an action is repeated several hundred times, the human brain naturally processes it through the lens of mechanical optimization (resource conservation, sightlines, cooldowns) rather than ethical gravity.
- Thus, the game constantly teeters between **profound moral shock** during scripted narrative confrontations and **routine mechanical desensitization** during open-world stealth-action arenas.

### Conclusion: The Forced Mirror
Ultimately, *The Last of Us Part II* uses interactive violence not to celebrate lethality, but to make the player feel the physical and emotional exhaustion of the cycle of violence. By the time Ellie and Abby collapse in the shallow surf of Santa Barbara—emaciated, weeping, bleeding, and mutilated—the player does not want to win. The player wants the controller to stop vibrating. The victory of *The Last of Us Part II* lies in achieving the rarest feat in interactive entertainment: **making the player genuinely, desperately want to lay down their weapons and stop playing**.
